One Item Away!

How a single progress bar turned JioMart’s cart into a goal worth chasing, and a coupon feature into a reward engine for multiple business outcomes

App name / Client
JioMart (Reliance Retail)
My Role
Product Designer (0-1 discovery & dev handoff)
Industry
Quick Commerce (B2C)
Platform
JioMart App (iOS & Android)

A JioMart shopper fills her basket with the week’s groceries.

She’s ₹50 short of a discount she’ll never know existed, because nothing on the screen tells her. She checks out, the business loses the larger basket and she loses the saving. Nobody did anything wrong, the interface just stayed silent at a critical time of the user’s journey. This is the story of how we broke that silence, and the calls we had to make to do it without cheapening the brand or breaking the margin model.

Every number here is a pre-launch target, labelled as such. The thinking is the point.

Chapter 1“You cant nudge someone toward a goal they cant see”

The brief said, “Increase Average Order Value”

But the real question was sharper, “What would make someone choose to add one more thing, in the moment, without feeling pushed?”

In Indian e-grocery, margins are thin and AOV is the most efficient lever there is. A bigger basket spreads fixed picking, packing and delivery costs across more items. JioMart had the inventory breadth across Grocery, Quick Commerce, Electronics, Fashion and Home. What it didn’t have was a reason, mid-session, for any of it to matter to the shopper.

Three leaks hidden in plain sight:

  • Flat baskets: People bought what they came for and left. Nothing made “one more item” feel worth it.
  • Wasted promo spend: Coupons sat in a generic tray, usually found at checkout and wasn’t changing behaviour.
  • Siloed verticals: A grocery run never hinted that Electronics or Fashion existed in the same trip.

So I reframed the goal as a feeling, not a metric: make the next rupee of spend feel like visible, earned progress toward a reward you can see, trust, and claim. Get that right, and AOV stops being something we extract from people. It becomes something they reach for.

Chapter 2Reading the Indian Shopper

It was important because the same mechanic can read as “generous” in one market and “gimmick” in another one.

Before opening Figma, I sat with how this audience actually behaves:

  • Value-maximisation is a sport here. Indian shoppers hunt deals and take real pride in extracting maximum value.
  • Trust lives in exact rupees. “Up to 30% off” reads as a catch. “₹50 off” reads as a promise. That single observation shaped one of my biggest trade-offs (next chapter).
  • The cart is a household item. An online basket is often a family run - groceries, a phone accessory, a kitchen item. Tracking each category’s reward independently mirrors how families actually shop, so a cross-category nudge feels natural rather than salesy, if done right.

The principle I designed around: In this market, honesty is the conversion mechanic. The interface never says “up to,” never hides the math. It states the precise gap and the precise reward at every step, because transparency is what unlocks the spend.

Chapter 3The First Thing I Got Wrong

My first instinct felt obviously right… Show people the whole prize wall. Every coupon they could eventually unlock in a category, laid out, so they’d aim high. More goals, more motivation. Right?

Wrong.

Faced with a spread of distant rewards, people couldn’t tell which one was closest, mis-judged the effort, and disengaged from the bar entirely. Choice overload, in one screen. Engineering, separately, flagged the same concept as a heavy, chatty payload that fought the performance budget. Two signals, one conclusion.

  • The call? Kill the prize wall. Show one goal at a time: the nearest unlocked reward per category, revealing the next tier only after the current one is won.
  • The trade-off? I gave up the “aspirational ceiling”. Users no longer see the biggest possible reward up front, which in theory could pull bigger baskets.
  • Why it held? The goal-gradient effect is real: motivation climbs as a visible goal gets closer. One reachable target beat ten distant ones in confusion.

That pivot taught me the spine of the whole feature: clarity converts, abundance paralyses.

The thread through all five: ship the simplest thing that can prove the bet, and protect trust and margin while doing it. Each “no” was a deliberate sequencing choice, not an omission.

The second course correction

Auto-apply nearly shipped. It was the “obvious” delight by quietly choosing the best coupon, maximise the visible saving. Two things stopped it. Technically, an optimiser was brittle against the real-time auto-revert logic. Experientially, a discount the user didn’t consciously choose reads as a gimmick in exactly the market that values transparency most.

Chapter 5Building It So It Could Grow

A feature touching Home, PLP, PDP, Shop All and Cart is a systems problem before it’s a visual one.

I brought Engineering in from week one, and we made a decision that paid off far beyond V1: one floating bar, not five bespoke layouts.

The bar hydrates from a single source of truth, so a coupon unlocked on the listing page reads as unlocked in the cart. Thresholds, rupee values and names all live in a CMS, so the business can change an offer without a code release. We designed templates, not screenshots.

Chapter 6How We’d Know It Worked

It was important that the project was not just shippable but measurable as well. (Since I am not authorised to disclose the actual numbers, here’s the direction I would take in order to prove whether the feature worked)

Some of the key factors we wanted to funnel and observe were as follows:

  • Average Order Value (AOV): A bigger basket was the strongest indicator to prove the worth of this feature
  • Coupon apply rate: Percentage increase in the number of users who ‘unlocked and used’ the coupons
  • Drop-off: Number of users who did not checkout or complete the payment

Every call was evidence-led: spot the drop-off, find the cause, fix it, then measure again so the next decision is data, not opinion (or a hunch!)

Chapter 7What It Taught Me

My key take-aways from the project and what I learnt

  • I shipped a system, not a screen: The bar was never the point. The reusable unlock engine was, which is exactly why the next chapter was possible.
  • Constraints sharpened the design: The half-a-second bar updation and the backend configuration mandate forced the single component, single source-of-truth architecture (the one I’m most proud of!)
  • Progressive disclosure: ‘Nearest-goal-only’ and ‘user-picks-we-bank’ were some major enlightenment in ‘human behaviour and psychology’
  • Transparency is the key: Especially for Indian consumers the exact rupees, visible rules, honest auto-revert were major head-turners

Chapter 8The Bigger Idea I Couldn’t Unsee

Somewhere in the build, a quieter realisation arrived: the coupon was never special. It was just the first thing we’d plugged into the slot. The progress bar, the threshold logic, the celebration, the bottom sheet - none of it cares whether the reward is a coupon. Swap the payload, keep the mechanic, and the same feature unlocks entirely new business levers.

That’s when a coupon feature became, in my head, a reward engine. Three extensions I scoped on that insight, each solving a different, real problem while reusing the exact same scaffold at near-zero incremental cost.

  • Free Delivery Unlock: Swap the coupon reward with ‘Free Delivery’ reward -the same progress bar now pushes the user to add more to waive off the fee
  • Free Gift Unlock: A ‘reward’ that hid in a particular category alone (Beauty), now became an upfront nudge on the homepage for users to shop more
  • Merging ‘Steal Deal’ feature: It blended AOV uplift with clearance and urgency, while the earned-access framing protects everyday price perception

Chapter 9The Bottomline

ONE engine. FOUR reward types. FOUR business outcomes. And, JUST ONE component to maintain in the library! A solid difference between shipping a feature and building a platform.
