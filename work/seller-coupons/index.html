Enabling Seller-Funded Coupons in JioMart

Led product design of a self-serve coupon system that helped sellers gain control, trust ROI, and scale participation in a high-volume quick-commerce platform.

App name / Client
JioMart - Reliance Retail
My Role
Product Designer
Industry
Quick Commerce (B2B2C)
Platform
Web - Seller Portal

TL;DR

Problem: Sellers avoided funding coupons due to uncertainty, lack of control, and poor ROI visibility.

Outcome:

  • Seller participation in coupons increased significantly within the first few months
  • Noticeable rise in repeat opt-ins across campaigns
  • Rise in self-serve adoption by sellers, reduction in platform-funded coupons
  • Potentially scalable model for business
  • Majority of campaigns moved to self-serve, reducing operational dependency
  • Clear improvement in seller confidence and engagement with performance data

1.Context

JioMart operates in a highly competitive quick-commerce ecosystem where discounts strongly influence buyer conversion. While platform-funded discounts worked in the short term, they were not financially scalable.

To unlock sustainable growth, sellers needed to partially or fully fund coupon campaigns themselves. But adoption was low.

2.The real problem

Through internal data analysis and seller conversations, we realised the issue wasn’t reluctance to spend.

Sellers were not anti-discount, they were “anti-uncertainty”.

Key signals:

  • Sellers dropped off after one campaign due to unclear outcomes
  • Performance data existed, but was fragmented and hard to interpret
  • Sellers felt they were “spending blindly” without control or predictability

Coupons were perceived as a black box, not a strategic lever.

3.Why this was a hard problem

This wasn’t a simple UI problem.

We were designing for:

  • Sellers with vastly different digital maturity levels
  • Catalog sizes ranging from a few SKUs to thousands
  • Financial anxiety tied directly to personal business outcomes

At the same time:

  • Business wanted scale and reduced ops effort
  • Category managers wanted targeted spend
  • Ops teams were manually nudging sellers to participate

Any solution had to balance trust, control, and scalability, without increasing complexity.

4.My role & ownership

  • Problem framing with Business stakeholders, PMs and Operations.
  • Participated in problem identification and strategic design with stakeholders
  • Research synthesis
  • UX strategy and interaction design
  • Dashboard and system-level design decisions
  • Iteration based on feedback post-rollout

5.The strategy

A simple, “Trust - Control - Feedback” rule

This translated into three design principles:

  • Trust | Transparency over persuasion. Show outcomes clearly, don’t oversell.
  • Control | Control over coercion. Let sellers choose how and when to participate.
  • Feedback | Progressive disclosure over data overload. Show what matters first, details on demand.

Rather than solving adoption through nudges, we decided to solve the underlying uncertainty.

6.Decision 1: Making coupon opt-in feel safe

Problem

Sellers saw coupons as risky because opt-in felt irreversible and outcomes were unclear.

Design decision

We redesigned the opt-in flow to feel low-risk and reversible:

  • Flexible budget input instead of fixed commitments
  • Clear visibility into what the coupon would apply to
  • Explicit opt-in / opt-out states

Why this worked

By framing opt-in as a controllable action rather than a commitment, sellers felt safer experimenting.

What we avoided

Aggressive urgency nudges or forced defaults, which would have increased short-term adoption but reduced trust.

Coupon design (some micro-decisions taken along the way)

Before: Sellers avoided funding coupons due to lack of control, and poor ROI visibility and uncertainty.

After: Reframed information around participation status, time window, and budget usage to improve clarity, and helped drive decision making.

7.Decision 2: Reframing performance through ROI visibility

Raw metrics and tables increased cognitive load and anxiety.
Even when sellers opted in, they couldn’t easily answer: “Is this working for me?”

Design decision

We designed a lightweight performance dashboard focused on:

  • Spend vs Budget as the primary mental model
  • Percentages over absolute numbers for quick comprehension
  • Real-time status and participation indicators
  • Progressive drill-down for power users

Why this worked

The dashboard didn’t just inform, it reassured.

Sellers could now:

  • See exactly how much they were spending
  • Track performance without needing interpretation
  • Feel in control of outcomes rather than reacting after the fact

8.What I intentionally did not design

To preserve trust and long-term adoption, we consciously avoided:

  • Dark patterns or misleading nudges
  • Over-automation of budget recommendations early on
  • Overloading sellers with advanced metrics upfront

The system prioritised confidence first, optimisation later.

9.Outcomes

Instead of absolute metrics, the impact is in directional and behavioural terms.

Seller behaviour

  • Strong increase in first-time and repeat participation
  • Sellers began opting into multiple campaigns over time

Business & Ops

  • Reduced manual nudging by ops teams
  • Majority of campaigns running self-serve

Platform maturity

  • Fewer configuration errors
  • More predictable coupon performance patterns

These outcomes were tracked via internal dashboards, ops feedback, and rollout comparisons across time periods.

10.Key learnings

  • Emotional blockers (fear, uncertainty) often matter more than functional gaps
  • Trust is built through predictable feedback loops, not persuasion
  • Removing complexity is often more impactful than adding features

11.What I’d do next

With trust established, the next phase would focus on:

  • AI-assisted budget and optimisation suggestions
  • Cross-campaign comparison views for smarter allocation
  • Deeper confidence signals tied to historical performance

12.Final reflection

This project reinforced how product design can act as a growth lever when it balances user psychology, business goals, and system scalability and not just visual polish.

Fin.
